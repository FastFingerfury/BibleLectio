Where the text comes from

2 Baruch.

R. H. Charles · 1913 · one book, 87 chapters.

A book in the name of Baruch, the prophet Jeremiah’s scribe, not the short Book of Baruch in the Apocrypha. Baruch sees Jerusalem fall to Babylon, mourns over Zion, fasts and prays, and God answers him: twelve woes to come on the earth, then the Messiah, a great harvest and the dead raised; a vision of a forest, a vine and a cedar; and a cloud that rains black and bright waters by turns, the story of the world from Adam to the Messiah. It ends with his letter to the nine and a half tribes far away, tied to an eagle’s neck. Jews wrote it about AD 100, some thirty years after the Romans had destroyed Jerusalem and its Temple in AD 70. The whole book comes down to us in one Syriac copy of the 500s or 600s, kept in Milan, translated from the Greek; its letter was also copied into many Syriac Bibles.

Whose words.

R. H. Charles’s English, from the Syriac, as he corrected it for his Oxford Apocrypha and Pseudepigrapha of the Old Testament of 1913, the same book as the app’s Jubilees, Twelve Patriarchs, 2 Enoch and Psalms of Solomon. He made the first English of the book in 1896; every free English of it is his. Public domain everywhere.

Every free English translation of 2 Baruch is R. H. Charles’s, from the Syriac. He made the first in 1896, a book of its own with long notes, and went over it again for his Oxford Apocrypha and Pseudepigrapha of 1913, after a leaf of the book in Greek, found in Egypt, had been published in 1903. The 1913 is his own corrected version, and the one the app gives. His small book of 1917, for the S.P.C.K., reprints the 1896 English with the 1913 corrections put in by its editors, though not all of them: it has the 1913’s “shall have kept” (29:4), but still “one vine” where the 1913 has “each (?) vine” (29:5). The translations scholars use today, A. F. J. Klijn’s of 1983 and D. M. Gurtner’s of 2009, are still under copyright.

How the words were settled.

No proofread digital copy exists, so we read the printed book: four scanned copies of the 1913 pages, each word settled by their majority, with Charles’s small book of 1917, nearly the same English, as a fifth witness. The verse numbers are his. His page prints them in the margin, against the line a verse begins in, and the 1917 book prints them in the text, so the two together show where each verse begins; every place where they disagree, and every word that could be a slip, was read on the page pictures.

No proofread digital copy of the 1913 translation exists, so the text was read from the printed pages, in four scans, each read by its own OCR. The footnotes, the running heads, the headings over the book’s parts and the signs in Charles’s margin for its sources were set aside, and on pages 487 to 489, where he prints his English of the Greek leaf and the Greek itself beside his English of the Syriac, only the first column was kept. The four readings and the 1917 book were lined up word by word and voted on every word. Where the scans split, or all four read a word that is no word, the 1917 book decided, but only where its word is the same word (“thou”, which every scan read as “thcu”), never where its editors kept the 1896 English. Every place where the text and the 1917 book then differ, 750 in the words, was read through: nearly all are the two books’ own, the 1913 English being the more modern (“will”, “has” and “forgets” where the 1917 book has “shall”, “hath” and “forgetteth”), and every one that could be a slip was read on the page pictures. Seven slips were put right as the book prints them: “law”, read as “Jaw” (3:6 and 84:8); “in Babylon”, whose dagger the scans read as a t, “tin” (33:2); “long-suffering”, broken at a line’s end (12:4); and three quotation marks the scans lost or misread (10:3, 42:2, 42:8).

The verse numbers.

Charles’s own, the ones used everywhere: 87 chapters, 696 verses. His 1913 page prints each chapter’s number in heavy figures and each verse’s in the margin, against the line the verse begins in; his 1917 book prints them in the text, before each verse’s first word. So each verse begins where the 1917 book begins it, and the 1913 margins check it: every place where the two disagree was read on the page pictures. Five verses begin where the 1913 page shows them to, not where the 1917 book does: 21:7, 21:21, 32:3, 54:4 and 54:19. 21:21 begins inside a line, with a small letter, as printed: “that Thy might may be made known”. Five verses have no number on the 1913 page, which runs them on from the verse before (1:5, 44:2, 67:9, 76:5 and 83:23); they begin where the 1917 book begins them. 27:8, the seventh of the twelve woes, is lost in the Syriac (Charles prints “[Wanting.]”), so the app goes from 27:7 to 27:9.

What is left out.

His footnotes, the headings over the book’s parts, and the signs in his margin for the sources he found it made of. His daggers round words he judged corrupt, and the thick type of his own corrections, the words staying. His square brackets stay, round words he judged added to the book later, and where they run over several verses each verse shows them; so do his round brackets, round words he supplied, his angle brackets, shown as round ones, and his (?) after a word he was unsure of. 27:8, the seventh woe, is lost in the Syriac, so the app goes from 27:7 to 27:9. The Greek of 12:1 to 14:3, found on a papyrus in Egypt, which he prints beside his English. A. F. J. Klijn’s translation of 1983 and the ones after it, as they are still under copyright.
