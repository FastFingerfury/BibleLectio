Where the text comes from

Twelve Patriarchs.

R. H. Charles · 1913 · 12 books, 142 chapters.

The last words of Jacob’s twelve sons to their children, one testament each: each confesses his sins, urges a virtue and tells of the last days. Kept in Greek, Armenian and Slavonic. Charles held it a Jewish book of the second century before Christ, with Christian sentences added later.

Whose words.

R. H. Charles’s English of 1913, in the Oxford Apocrypha and Pseudepigrapha of the Old Testament, the same book as the app’s Jubilees and Testament of Moses. Public domain everywhere.

Charles translated the Testaments from the Greek in 1908, with the Armenian and Slavonic versions beside it, and revised his translation for the 1913 volume. He died in 1931, so it is public domain everywhere; the modern translations (H. C. Kee’s of 1983, Hollander and de Jonge’s of 1985) are in copyright. The copies that go round online are Rutherford Platt’s reprint in The Forgotten Books of Eden (1926): Charles’s words with his brackets dropped, a word touched up here and there (“I eat” made “I ate”), and the testaments cut into chapters and verses of Platt’s own. The words here are Charles’s, with his own chapters and verses, the ones books about the Testaments quote: Levi 18:2 is Levi 18:2.

Where we read it.

  • The 1913 volume on archive.org, the University of Toronto’s scan (apocryphapseudep02charuoft): the printed pages 296 to 360, whose pictures were read by eye
  • A second scan of it (apocryphapseudep0002unse)
  • A third (apocryphapseudep0002rhch_w1w3)
  • A fourth (TheApocryphaAndPseudepigraphaOfTheOldTestamentInEnglishWith_439)
  • A fifth (Charles_The-Apocrypha-of-the-Old-Testament-vol-2_1913)
  • Charles’s own edition of 1917 for the SPCK, his translation again with each verse’s number in the line, where the verse begins (testamentsoftwel00char)
  • scrollmapper’s bible_databases_deuterocanonical on GitHub, a typed copy of Platt’s reprint: a witness to the words only

How the words were settled.

No proofread digital copy exists, so we read the printed book: five scanned copies of the same pages, each word settled by their majority, the typed copy online as one more voice, and the doubtful places read on the page pictures. The chapter and verse numbers are Charles’s own, from his edition of 1917, each checked against the 1913 margin.

No proofread digital copy of the 1913 translation exists, so the text was read from the printed pages, in five scans, each read by its own OCR. In about forty places Charles prints a second or third text beside the first, with a rule between the columns; the rules were found on the page pictures and only the left column was kept. The five readings and the typed copy were lined up word by word and voted on every word, the typed copy never on a bracket, since Platt dropped them; a tie went to the first scan, or, where it and the typed copy differed in a stop alone, to the typed copy. Seven slips the vote could not settle were read on the page pictures: a comma whose tail did not print, a hyphen lost at a line’s end, the printer’s “An” for “And” (Levi 3:8), two daggers the OCR read as letters, and two square brackets. Charles’s own spellings stay: “eat” for “ate”, “connexion”, “mightst”, “alway”.

The verse numbers.

Charles’s: twelve testaments, 142 chapters, 993 verses. The 1913 book prints a verse’s number in the margin, against the line the verse begins in; his 1917 book prints it in the line, where the verse begins. Each verse here begins where the 1917 book begins it, checked against the 1913 margin, and where the two differ, or the 1917 copy lost a number, the 1913 margin decides. Where the left column lacks a verse the column beside it has (Zebulun 4:4, Gad 2:4, Joseph 19:3 to 7), its number is left out.

What is left out.

Where Charles prints two or three texts side by side, only the left one is given, as in his 1917 edition, so a verse found only beside it is not here (Zebulun 4:4, Gad 2:4, Joseph 19:3 to 7). His footnotes, daggers and corner marks are left out. His square brackets stay, round the words he judged added later, and his round brackets, round words he supplied.
