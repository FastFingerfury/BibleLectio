Where the text comes from

Psalms 151 to 155.

Brenton 1851 · W. Wright 1887 · one book, 5 chapters.

Five psalms beyond the 150 of the Bible. Psalm 151, David’s own song of the shepherd boy who was anointed and killed Goliath, comes right after Psalm 150 in the Greek Bible, and the Orthodox churches keep it. Psalms 152 to 155 come down to us in Syriac copies, kept with Psalm 151: two more of David, on the lion and the wolf, a call to praise and a prayer for mercy. Three of the five, 151, 154 and 155, were also found in Hebrew among the Dead Sea Scrolls.

Whose words.

Psalm 151 in the English of Bagster’s Greek and English Septuagint, printed in London in 1851 and known by Sir Lancelot Brenton’s name, the book of the app’s 3 and 4 Maccabees. Psalms 152 to 155 in William Wright’s English of 1887, from the Syriac. Public domain everywhere.

Psalm 151 is given as the Greek Bible has it, in the same old “thou” English as the app’s King James Apocrypha and 3 and 4 Maccabees. Psalms 152 to 155 have one free English translation, Wright’s: a professor of Arabic at Cambridge, he printed them in 1887 in the Proceedings of the Society of Biblical Archaeology, from a Syriac copy at Cambridge, with his English. He translated a Syriac Psalm 151 too, a verse longer than the Greek; the Greek one was chosen, as the one the Greek Bible and the Orthodox churches keep.

Where we read it.

  • ebible.org’s typed copy of Brenton’s book (eng-Brenton), public domain, the words of Psalm 151 in the app
  • A Bagster reprint of the book of about 1900 on archive.org (septuagintversio1900bren), whose page of Psalm 151 was read by eye
  • A second scan of it (septuagintversio0000unse_g6n1)
  • A scan of another printing (brenton-lancelot-the-septuagint-with-apocrypha-old-testament-english-1851-impresso)
  • William Wright, “Some Apocryphal Psalms in Syriac”, Proceedings of the Society of Biblical Archaeology 9 (1887), pages 257 to 266, on archive.org (ProceedingsOfTheSocietyOfBiblicalArchaeologyVol091887): the words of Psalms 152 to 155 in the app, his English on pages 264 to 266
  • A second scan of the same volume (proceedingssoci05archgoog), whose pictures of pages 264 to 266 were read by eye

How the words were settled.

Psalm 151 from the typed copy of Brenton’s book on ebible.org, read against three scanned copies of the printed page. Psalms 152 to 155 have no typed copy, so we read two scanned copies of Wright’s pages, and every word on the page pictures; fourteen slips of the scans were put right. Each psalm’s title stands in square brackets at the head of its first verse, as in the app’s King James psalms.

Psalm 151: the typed copy agrees word for word with the three scans of Brenton’s page. Its title names Goliath “Goliad”, as Brenton prints it from the Vatican manuscript he followed; his footnote gives “Goliath” from the Alexandrian one. Psalms 152 to 155: the words were taken from the OCR text of the first scan of Wright’s pages, compared word by word with the second scan’s, and every word was read on the page pictures. Fourteen places were put right as the journal prints them: “surrounded”, “O” and “I”, which the OCR read as “surrotmded”, “0” and “1”; “of”, whose f is broken on the page in both copies; two round brackets read as braces; two of Wright’s footnote marks and two stray marks the OCR took into the words; two commas it dropped (“the wolf, which took a sheep”, “the wolf, and he had slain”); and “Sheōl”, which Wright prints with a line over the o (Psalms 152:2 and 153:3). His own spellings stay: “honour”, “well nigh”.

The verse numbers.

Brenton’s for Psalm 151, 7 verses. Wright’s for the others, as he numbers each psalm’s verses: Psalms 152 and 153 have 6 each, 154 has 17 and 155 has 20; 56 verses in all. Wright prints the five in the order of the Syriac copies, I to V; scholars now number them 151, 154, 155, 152 and 153, and so does the app: his II is Psalm 154, his III Psalm 155, his IV Psalm 152 and his V Psalm 153.

What is left out.

The Greek and the Syriac the two books print with the English, their footnotes, and Brenton’s italics for a word the translator supplied, as in the app’s King James Bible; Wright’s own brackets in the verses stay. Wright’s Psalm 151, the Syriac one, a verse longer: the app gives the Greek one. The longer Hebrew Psalm 151 of the Dead Sea Scrolls, as every English translation of it is still under copyright.
