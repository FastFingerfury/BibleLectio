Where the text comes from

Testament of Moses.

R. H. Charles · 1913 · the ending is lost · one book, 12 chapters.

Moses’s last words to Joshua, foretelling Israel’s story to the end of days: a Jewish book of the first century, kept in one Latin manuscript of the fifth or sixth century in Milan, which breaks off in chapter 12, so the ending is lost.

Whose words.

R. H. Charles’s English of 1913, in the Oxford Apocrypha and Pseudepigrapha of the Old Testament. Public domain everywhere.

The book survives in one manuscript only, a Latin palimpsest of the fifth or sixth century in the Ambrosian Library in Milan, found and printed by Ceriani in 1861: a translation of a Greek translation of a Hebrew or Aramaic original of the first century. Charles’s 1913 translation revises his 1897 book, the standard English of its time, and is public domain everywhere; the accurate modern translations are in copyright and cannot be used. Charles himself judged the book the Testament of Moses, not the Assumption, and his first verse opens “The Testament of Moses”.

Where we read it.

  • The 1913 volume on archive.org (apocryphapseudep02charuoft): its PDF pages 434 to 444 are the printed pages 414 to 424
  • A second, independent scan of the same edition on archive.org (apocryphapseudep0002unse)
  • Charles’s 1897 book, The Assumption of Moses, on archive.org (assumptionofmose00unknuoft), for where each verse begins

How the words were settled.

Typed from the printed pages of the 1913 book (pages 414 to 424), read from two scans of them on archive.org and then, line by line, against the page pictures. Each verse begins where Charles’s Latin text of 1897 begins it, as the 1913 book prints the numbers in the margin only.

No proofread digital copy of the 1913 translation exists, so the text was taken from the printed pages: cut out of each scan’s OCR text, the notes dropped, the two readings compared word by word, and then every line read against the page pictures of the first scan. The OCR’s slips were of the usual kind, a letter dropped, punctuation moved, and none of them is in the file.

The verse numbers.

The 1913 book prints a verse’s number in the margin, against the line the verse begins in, not at the word. Charles’s 1897 book prints his Latin text with the numbers in the line, so each verse here begins where that Latin begins it. Twelve chapters, 134 verses, the last breaking off where the manuscript does.

What is left out.

His notes, his thick type and his italics. His brackets stay: round brackets for words he supplied or restored, square brackets for words he judged a later addition; a gap in the manuscript is shown as …
